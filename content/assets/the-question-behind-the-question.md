For the first few months, the first thing I asked when the boys came back was how their week had gone. How things were over there. What they did.

It sounded like interest. Any parent would ask it.

It was not interest. I was checking the scoreboard.

## What I was actually doing

I wanted to know what she was doing with them so I could measure it against what I was doing with them. Whether she took them somewhere I had not. Whether they came back happier than they left. Whether I needed to do something better.

I was in competition with her for my kids' affection, and I was using them to find out how I was doing.

Nobody told me I was doing it. I did not decide to do it. It just came out of my mouth every Sunday for months, dressed up as a normal question from a dad who missed his kids.

## How you know you are doing it

You are not listening to the answer. You are grading it.

Notice what happens in your chest when they say they had a great week. If the honest reaction is anything other than glad, you are not asking. You are collecting.

The other tell is the follow-up. A dad who is interested asks what the movie was about. A dad who is collecting asks who else was there.

## What I did instead

I stopped asking about the other house. Not softened it, not asked less often. Stopped.

I did not announce it and I did not make it a rule. I just quit opening with it.

What I kept doing was leaving the door open. If they wanted to tell me something, it landed somewhere safe. No reaction, no follow-up questions, no face.

## What happened

They still tell me things. Years later, they will bring up something they did over there that they are excited about, unprompted, because it is theirs and they want to share it.

That is the whole trade. You give up the weekly briefing and you get the thing you actually wanted, which is your kids talking to you because they feel like it.

A kid who is not being interviewed will report. A kid who is being interviewed learns to manage the interview.

## What it costs if you do not stop

You are making them messengers. They will figure out that what they say about one house lands in the other, and they will start editing. First to protect their mother, then to protect you, and eventually they are managing both of you and telling neither of you anything true.

That is the part that turns into feeling like a visitor. Not the schedule. Not the house. The moment your kids start handling you.

## The practical version

**Stop opening with it.** The first question when they walk in is about them, right now, not about the last three days somewhere else. "You hungry" is a complete question.

**Let the silence sit.** They will fill it eventually, with something you did not think to ask about.

**When they do volunteer something, give it nothing.** No follow-up, no probing, no expression you have to manage. Just take it like any other thing your kid told you.

**If you slip, do not make a thing of it.** No apology, no explanation. Just do not ask the next one.

## One more thing

The urge behind the fishing is not bad. It is that you miss them and you are scared you are losing them.

That does not go away by getting better information. It goes away by being the same guy every time they walk through your door, for long enough that they stop bracing for it.

Consistency is the thing I am still working on. It is also the only thing that has ever worked.
