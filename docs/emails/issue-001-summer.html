This week's reset

Summer is the hardest season. Here’s why — and what helps.

Nobody warns you about summer.

During the school year, there’s a rhythm. Pickup times are fixed. Drop-offs are predictable. The schedule does a lot of the communication work for you — and when there’s less to coordinate, there’s less opportunity for things to go sideways.

Then summer arrives.

Camps change week to week. Activities shift. Plans that were set two months ago suddenly need to be renegotiated. The number of texts and emails between co-parents doubles — sometimes triples — and with more interactions comes more surface area for misunderstanding, misreading, and miscommunication.

If summer feels harder than the rest of the year, it’s not you. It’s the season.

Why summer escalates tension

More decisions = more contact. Every camp, activity, and schedule change requires coordination. Each one is a potential flashpoint if either parent is already stressed.

Unstructured time changes kids’ behavior. Kids feel the lack of routine too. That can show up as acting out — which can look like a co-parenting problem when it’s really just a kid-needs-more-structure problem.

Vacations and travel create new pressure. Requesting time outside the normal schedule — even reasonably — can feel threatening to the other parent. Timing and framing matter more than usual.

The antidote isn’t less communication. It’s better-positioned communication.

One thing that helps: before you respond to anything summer-related, ask yourself one question first — what does my kid actually need here?

Not what’s fair. Not what you agreed to. Not what the other parent should or shouldn’t be doing. Just: what does my kid need?

That question reorients everything. It’s harder to dig in on a position when you’re genuinely thinking about the child at the center of it.

This week’s message script

When a summer schedule request comes in that you weren’t expecting, try this before you respond:

“Thanks for letting me know. Let me look at what we have on our end and I’ll get back to you by [specific day]. I want to make sure [child’s name] has a good summer.”

What this does: buys you time to respond thoughtfully instead of reactively, confirms you’re looking at the calendar (not stonewalling), and puts the kid’s name in the message — which subtly reminds both of you what this is actually about.

You don’t have to have an open mind about everything. But going into summer-related conversations with the assumption that the other parent also wants the kids to have a good summer — even if everything else between you is hard — tends to produce better outcomes than the alternative.

Give it a try this week. Notice what happens.

Track your calm this summer. One day at a time.
